# UX Design Principles

## Why this exists

Code can be correct and still fail the person using it. Confusing flows, unclear wording, and dead ends
make people give up, even when everything "works." Good UX is what makes a product feel trustworthy and
effortless. As the designer in the room, this is where your judgment is the standard.

## The main idea

Design for the real person, not the ideal one. Assume they are busy, distracted, and new. Make the next
step obvious, make mistakes easy to recover from, and never make them feel stupid.

## The standard to follow

Apply **Nielsen Norman Group's 10 Usability Heuristics** (Jakob Nielsen's widely-used usability
standard). For each change, name the specific heuristic it supports, for example: visibility of system
status, match between the system and the real world, user control and freedom, error prevention,
recognition rather than recall, and help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors. Tie the
design choice to a named heuristic, not personal taste.

## The checklist

1. **Clarity over cleverness.** Use plain, human language. Label buttons with what they do ("Save
   draft"), not vague words ("Submit").
2. **Obvious next step.** On every screen, the primary action should be the most visible thing. One
   clear path, not five competing ones.
3. **Forgiving by default.** Let people undo, go back, and edit. Confirm before anything destructive.
   Never punish a wrong tap.
4. **Helpful errors.** Say what went wrong and how to fix it, in plain words. Not "Error 422."
5. **Respect states.** Design the empty state, the loading state, and the error state, not just the
   happy path.
6. **Reduce effort.** Remove steps, fields, and choices that are not needed. The best interface asks
   the least of the person.

## How to prove it

For each change, name who the user is, what they are trying to do, and how this design makes that
easier or safer. Tie the decision to a real user need, not personal taste.
